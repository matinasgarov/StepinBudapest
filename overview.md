# StepIn Budapest — Project Overview

## What this is

A single-page website for **StepIn Budapest**, a service that helps Azerbaijani students move to Budapest — meeting them at the airport, finding safe housing, handling residence paperwork, and getting them set up with the practical things (SIM card, bank account, transport pass).

The site exists to do **one thing**: get a family to open WhatsApp and start a conversation. Everything on the page is measured against that.

## Who it's for

The visitor we design for is a **parent in Azerbaijan**, not the student.

They are usually reading on a phone, in Azerbaijani or Russian, and they are about to send their child to a country they may never have visited. They are not shopping for a bargain — they are looking for a reason to trust strangers with someone they love.

That single fact drives most decisions on the page. The tone is calm rather than promotional. Claims are specific rather than enthusiastic. The design leans formal and established, because a page that feels casual would work against the thing being sold.

The student is a secondary reader. The page is written so a parent can understand the whole offer without their child translating it.

## What's on the page

The page reads as a sequence of chapters, alternating dark and light so each section feels like a distinct step.

**Opening** — The headline promise: *"Your student arrives ready. You sleep easy."* Set against a blue-hour photograph of Budapest across the Danube. Two ways to get in touch, and a note that replies come within hours in any of the three languages.

**The arrival plan** — The signature piece. A panel styled like a printed travel itinerary: Baku to Budapest, and the four stages of arrival — Pickup, Housing, Documents, Settled. It reveals itself step by step when the page loads. The idea is that the parent sees the whole journey already planned before they read another word.

**Proof strip** — A slow-moving band of reassurances: students helped, an Azerbaijani-speaking team, WhatsApp support, verified housing.

**Services** — The four things handled, described plainly.

**Process** — Three stages, from first message to arrival. This section advances as you scroll, so the process visibly moves rather than sitting still. It's the one moment on the page that demonstrates rather than describes.

**Pricing** — Quoted in USD: Basic 99, Comfort 119, Standard 299 (299, or 235 without guardianship) and Premium 469, with Standard marked as most popular. A build-your-own row composes a WhatsApp message from tickboxes. The AZN figures this document used to quote are gone from the site.

**FAQ** — The five questions parents actually ask, including the anxious ones: *Will someone be with my child when they land? How do I know they're safe? Can I contact you directly?*

**Contact** — WhatsApp as the primary route, with Instagram and email as alternatives.

A WhatsApp button stays visible in the corner throughout, so the parent never has to hunt for the way in.

## Languages

The site runs in **English, Azerbaijani, and Russian**. It picks the visitor's language automatically on first visit and remembers the choice for next time. A single button in the top-right switches between them.

All three are complete — every word on the page is translated, not just the headlines.

## The look

Deep navy and gold, with an editorial serif for headings and a typewriter-style face for codes, prices, and labels. The gold is used sparingly and always as an accent — a rule, a numeral, a single highlight — never as a large coloured block.

The recurring visual idea is the **travel document**: airport codes, ruled lines, stamps, step numbers. It appears in the arrival plan, the process section, and the logo itself, which is three ascending arches — Budapest's architecture, read as steps, with the last one gold for the destination.

Movement is used to reinforce meaning rather than for decoration: the itinerary filling in, the process advancing as you scroll, the count of students climbing. Visitors who prefer reduced motion get a still version of everything.

## Worth considering later

- **Real photographs** of the team meeting students at the airport would do more for trust than any design choice. A parent wants to see the faces.
- **Named team members** — even first names and a photo — turn an anonymous service into people.
- **A specific response-time promise**, if it can be kept.
- **Proof of the housing vetting** — what "verified" actually involves, in a sentence or two.
